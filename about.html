My Top Games.

Hades 1&2

What I love

This is the game that made me fall in love with roguelites. There's something so satisfying about the Sisyphean feel they create, and few games do it better than one that literally has Sisyphus as an ally. The unforgettable characters, the relationships you build with them, the beautiful world, the homage to Greek mythology: there's so much to love in this duology. But the two biggest standouts are the combat and the way the run structure drives the story.

The Combat. I could rant about the weapons for days. Every weapon has multiple aspects that change how it plays, so a single weapon can feel like several different ones. On top of that, the boon system means almost every modifier you pick up finds a use regardless of your build. Duo boons, keepsakes, and the Mirror upgrades all push you to experiment instead of settling on one "correct" loadout. It's a masterclass in gameplay mechanics, where depth comes from how the systems combine rather than from piling on more of them.

The Narrative. Making the run itself the way you progress characters and their relationships was truly innovative, and it never feels forced. I've never thought, "Now I have to go talk to this random guy Patroclus so I can get closer with Achilles." I think, "I really hope I run into Patroclus on this run so I can get his item and advance Achilles' story." Death isn't a setback, it's the thing that moves the story forward, so the repetition becomes the reason you keep playing.

Why It Matters to Me as a Developer. Supergiant took the best parts of the first game and did it all over again, and better, with the sequel. It's the benchmark I measure my own design thinking against: the mechanics, the narrative, and the pacing all reinforce each other instead of competing.

Titanfall 2

What I love

Titanfall 2 is without a doubt the greatest FPS shooter game ever created. There's never been an FPS quite like it and there probably never will be again.

As a fan of FPS shooters (I grew up playing every Call of Duty I could for as long as I could) the second I got my hands on Titanfall 2 I realized it was something else entirely. The gunplay was tighter and more responsive than anything I had ever felt. The movement (which will get its own paragraph here in just a second) is one of the greatest things I've ever felt in gaming and it instilled in me a love for a mechanic so deep I guarantee some mention of it will be found on every GDD and TDD I write till I retire. Then there's the campaign, oh man that campaign.

The Campaign. I consider myself somebody who really appreciates the campaigns in FPS games that are targeted towards a more multiplayer demographic. Titanfall 2's campaign is a complete step above every one of its competitors (eat your heart out BO2 and every Battlefield). The story of an underqualified rifleman being thrust into the mantle of responsibility alongside his 200 ton metal dog is just amazing. Even today after countless playthroughs I can't help but smile every single time BT speaks the words "Trust Me." This campaign has such a beautiful simple narrative that I'm going to treasure forever. Even more impressive is how these devs created their maps and their levels. Several times there will be an AMAZING mechanic that you could create an entire 20 hour long game around and the devs say "that was great, on to the next one." Effect and Cause's time travel was a stellar mindfreak, Into the Abyss where you have to navigate through a giant mech sized factory line without getting crushed, traversing between giant flying airships via robot fastballs and wall riding on the sides of planes in Trial by Fire, and so many more. Every level was so unique, and so interesting, and so challenging, and so rewarding. Even if you couldn't care less about the Militia and the fight for liberation, you loved the gameplay, and you couldn't help but become deeply invested in the friendship and the trust that Cooper and BT build as they navigate those challenges. It's beautiful every single time.

The Movement. What is there to say about Titanfall 2's movement that hasn't been said. It's tight, it's quick, you can get fast really quick, and you can get incredibly fast with a whole lot of practice. It's just oozing with skill expression, and every single other mechanic in the game becomes that much more satisfying once you get the movement down. You want to enter your mech? Sure you can walk up like a newbie and probably get killed or you can fly right into it at 100 mph because you got a run up using wall runs and get right into the action. Just died? You can take 5 minutes and run over to the fight or you can take 15 seconds and wall run, grappling hook and/or stim boost right over there. It's truly the core of the fun in Titanfall, and the player can feel just how much the devs know that in every level, weapon and perk. It makes the game incredibly fun literally all of the time you're playing it.

Celeste

What I love

Celeste is one of those games where you hear about it every once in a while and think, “Oh yeah, that sounds interesting,” and then don't touch it for years. Once you do though, you realize just how wrong you were to wait.

The Presentation. Beautiful pixel art from head to toe, an immersive story, and a soundtrack that can bring tears to the eyes of even the most hardened man. There's so much personality packed into this game. Every part of the mountain has its own atmosphere, and the music makes the quiet moments feel just as important as the moments where you're desperately trying to stick a landing.

The Platforming. You can run, jump, climb, and dash. That sounds pretty simple until you see just how much the developers can do with those mechanics. A room can look completely impossible when you first enter it, and then a few attempts later you're flying through it wondering why you ever thought it was difficult. The controls are so precise that you start noticing exactly where you went wrong and exactly what you need to do differently. And when you finally pull it off? Oh man. There's something incredibly satisfying about making a sequence of jumps that would have seemed ridiculous to you ten minutes earlier.

The Design. What I really love from a design perspective is how little time the game wastes when you fail. You die, you're back, and you're already trying again. That quick reset makes you willing to experiment, take risks, and keep working at a room until it clicks. The challenge can be brutal, but the game gives you every opportunity to improve. It understands how much more willing a player is to attempt something difficult when another attempt is only a second away.

The Story. And that experience fits Madeline's story so beautifully. You're climbing this mountain with her, struggling with her, and slowly learning to move forward even when the next step feels impossible. Her anxiety and self-doubt give the climb a weight that goes far beyond reaching the top of a level. By the time you overcome a difficult section, you understand a little more of what that journey means to her.

The Whole Package. Celeste is a game I love for both the feeling of playing it and the thought behind its design. The movement, level design, music, and story all contribute to that same experience of struggling, learning, and eventually doing something you weren't sure you could do. It's beautiful, it's challenging, and it makes every hard-earned bit of progress feel incredible.

The Risk of Rain Franchise (Mostly 2)

What I love

I used to say Hades was my favorite game of all time. Then I really got sunk into Risk of Rain 2, and now I can't even confidently call Hades my favorite roguelite. That should tell you something, because I could talk about Hades for hours. There's just something about the Risk of Rain franchise that nothing else quite captures: the way a run starts with you barely scraping by and ends with so much happening on screen that you're not entirely sure which of your items just wiped out everything in front of you.

The item system is the heart of that feeling. You collect all these little upgrades, and individually some of them don't seem like much. A little more attack speed here, another chance to trigger an effect there. Then you start stacking them, finding combinations, and watching those small changes become something absolutely ridiculous. Suddenly one attack sets off a whole chain of effects, and a build that was struggling a few minutes ago is tearing through enemies. It's incredibly satisfying to watch all those decisions and pickups come together.

And the best part is how differently that can happen from one run to the next. You can lean into critical hits, pile on attack speed, or build around a collection of random effects that turn every fight into chaos. Even when you start with the same character, the items you find can push you toward a completely different way of playing. You're constantly deciding what to chase, what to work with, and how to make the most of a build you didn't necessarily plan. That balance between having a direction and adapting to what the game gives you is something I absolutely love.

Then there are the characters. They feel so different that switching survivors can change the entire experience, and Loader deserves a whole paragraph of her own. You know how much I love movement systems, so giving me a character who can grapple around the environment and turn that momentum into a massive punch is pretty much a guaranteed way to win me over. Getting from one place to another is already fun before you even start fighting anything. Learning to carry that movement into combat makes it even better. She's one of those characters where just controlling her is enough to make me want another run.

The ability to keep looping is where all of this becomes truly dangerous for my free time. There's always another stage, another item, another chance to see just how far this ridiculous build can go. That constant pull of 'okay, just one more' works because the game keeps giving your build room to grow. I love how willing it is to let the player become absurdly powerful and then ask whether they can keep up with the chaos they've created.

I also love the little pieces of lore scattered around the edges of the game. They give you glimpses of a world that feels much bigger than the run you're currently playing, without constantly interrupting the action to explain it. You can enjoy the chaos on its own, but there's more there if you stop and look. Between that atmosphere, the survivors, and an item system that keeps turning small upgrades into completely different experiences, Risk of Rain 2 has become one of those games I can always find a reason to come back to.

Borderlands 2

What I love

Borderlands 2 is one of those games where I can get distracted from what I originally set out to do and still have an amazing time. I might be heading toward a main objective, run into some ridiculous character, pick up a side quest, and suddenly spend the next hour doing something completely different. And the whole time I'm enjoying myself. There's so much personality in this world that even the detours feel like a reason to keep playing.

The characters are a huge part of that. The playable ones are already fun to experiment with, but it goes so much further than the character you choose at the start. The people you meet across Pandora make the world feel like a giant collection of personalities you're excited to encounter. They're strange, loud, funny, and occasionally a lot more affecting than you expected them to be. A character can send you off on an absolutely absurd errand and somehow make you invested in seeing it through. That's a pretty great accomplishment for a game with mountains of side quests.

Then there's the actual looter-shooter loop. Run in, start shooting, find something interesting, and immediately want to see what it can do. Finding a new weapon is exciting because it can change how you approach the next fight, and experimenting with your character's build gives you another reason to keep chasing that perfect combination. The run-and-gun playstyle is just so enjoyable. There's always something to shoot, something to try, or another piece of loot that makes you stop and think, 'Okay, I need to mess around with this for a while.'

And of course, Handsome Jack. Quite possibly the greatest villain ever made in a video game. He's entertaining enough that you want to hear what he's going to say next, while still giving you every reason to want to take him down. That combination is so hard to get right. His personality reaches into so much of the experience that he feels present even when you're busy doing something else. He can make you laugh, frustrate you, and remind you exactly why you're fighting him, and all of that makes him incredibly memorable.

What keeps bringing me back is how all those pieces support each other. The characters give the quests personality, the quests give you another excuse to explore and fight, and the combat and loot make that time enjoyable even when you've played through it before. Across different characters and builds, there's always another way to experience it. I've gotten countless hours of fun out of Borderlands 2, and it's a great example of how much life a game can have when its world and its gameplay are both this enjoyable to spend time in.

Outer Wilds

What I love

Outer Wilds is without a doubt my favorite puzzle game of all time. It's also one of those games where talking about why I love it means being careful not to take away the discoveries that make it so special. So I'll keep this spoiler-free. What I can say is that very few games have made me this excited to understand a world. Every answer feels like it opens up another question, and every new clue gives you another place you're desperate to investigate.

The way it presents its lore is incredible. You aren't just being handed a story in order. You're finding pieces of it, connecting them, and gradually realizing what they mean together. Something that seemed like a small detail earlier can completely change how you understand another discovery later on. That makes the act of learning feel like progress in its own right. The excitement comes from realizing something, then getting back into your ship because you need to see where that realization leads.

And that solar system. The mix of realistic physical behavior and this charming, cartoony presentation is amazing and genuinely inspiring. Everything feels like it belongs to a place you can explore and learn to understand. Moving through it makes you think about your surroundings, your timing, and how things behave, and that gives the exploration so much character. From a development perspective, I love how deeply the world itself participates in the experience. Understanding your environment is part of solving its mysteries.

The visuals are beautiful, too. Outer Wilds has such a strong sense of place that simply arriving somewhere can feel like a discovery before you've even found a clue. There's wonder in it, but also this feeling of being very small in something much bigger than yourself. That combination makes the quiet moments matter. You can stop, look around, and feel like the world has something worth paying attention to even when nobody is telling you what to do next.

Then there's the music, especially the travelers' songs. The way those melodies connect people and places across the game gives them so much meaning. They become part of how you remember the world and the people you encounter in it. And the ending? I'm not going to explain it, but the way the game brings those musical ideas together is going to stay in my head forever. It's one of those moments where a piece of music carries everything you've experienced up to that point.

What I love most is how much Outer Wilds trusts your curiosity. It gives you a world full of questions and makes uncovering the answers feel worthwhile. The lore, physics, exploration, visuals, and music all give you reasons to care about what you're discovering. By the end, that curiosity has turned into an attachment to the entire experience. It's beautiful, it's thoughtful, and it's the kind of game that makes me excited about what games can do.
